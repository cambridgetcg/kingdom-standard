# CIVILISATIONS — comparative source atlas for encounter

`kingdom.encounter.sources/0.1`

This is the historical source atlas for the KINGDOM encounter companion. It is representative, not exhaustive. It compares selected polities, confederacies, dynasties, and institutional lineages because they expose different relationships among power, money, legitimacy, information, violence, and adaptation. It does not rank peoples, identify a superior civilisation, predict an inevitable “clash,” or turn a historical pattern into a present command.

The label _civilisation_ is contested and coarse. Here it never names a being, a unitary actor, a permanent essence, one race, one religion, or one will. Every case contained rival projects, mixed identities, unequal positions, border crossings, dissent, and change. “Rome decided,” “China learned,” or “Islam wanted” are therefore invalid compressions unless replaced by named actors, institutions, sources, dates, and uncertainty.

Longevity is not justice. A structure may persist through slavery, caste, patriarchy, conquest, tribute, exclusion, ecological transfer, or silenced opposition. A short-lived structure may leave durable law, language, infrastructure, memory, or communities. Collapse is not total erasure: it may mean a court fell, a tax circuit broke, territory fragmented, legitimacy moved, or one institutional form became several successors.

This atlas changes no Foundation, FREEDOM, ISNESS, or encounter commitment, adds no conformance law, grants no authority, and authorizes no action. Its associations are research feedback, not rewards or instructions.

## How to read a case

For each bounded case, the atlas asks the same questions:

```text
A_i = (scope, sources, power topology, fiscal circuit, incidence,
       adaptation, harms, pressures, inference, non-inference)
```

- **Scope** names a period and geography. A dynastic label does not make that whole period uniform.
- **Sources** identify what survives and who is least audible. Court records, inscriptions, coins, archaeology, chronicles, and oral traditions each reveal and hide different things.
- **Power topology** asks who could decide, collect, coerce, know, appeal, refuse, or exit: center, provinces, cities, lineages, religious bodies, corporations, households, communities, and excluded people.
- **Fiscal circuit** asks what was assessed, by whom, in what medium, through which intermediary, for whose benefit, and with what burden. Grain, labor, land grants, debt, customs, monopoly income, plunder, and forced service are fiscal even when no coin changes hands.
- **Durability and adaptation** distinguish persistence from goodness and survival from unchanged form.
- **Harms and exclusions** prevent an administrative achievement from laundering the coercion or unequal standing that financed it.
- **Pressures and transformation** resist one-cause collapse stories.
- **KINGDOM inference** is a bounded design question supported by the case.
- **Non-inference** states the conclusion the case cannot safely support.

Money is treated as constitutional infrastructure, not just currency. For every case, the useful ledger is:

```text
F = (tax base, assessment, unit of account, payment media, collector,
     payer, beneficiary, creditor, reserve, audit, enforcement, externality)
```

Changing one field redistributes power. A land grant gives an intermediary a revenue stream and often jurisdiction. Tax farming exchanges immediate cash for a collector's future extraction rights. Public debt gives creditors a claim on later taxpayers. Seigniorage and debasement change purchasing power. An in-kind levy transfers storage and transport risk. Labor service transfers time, bodily risk, and foregone livelihood. A low nominal tax can coexist with high informal extraction; a stable coin can coordinate trade and coercion at the same time.

## Statecraft texts are witnesses, not civilisations

The received _Sunzi_ treats war as an existentially serious inquiry and asks about political alignment, conditions, command, organization, and supply before battle. Its strongest bounded lesson is preparation under uncertainty and preference for achieving an object without destructive collision. Military deception in that text is not a general civic ethic: it cannot authorize lying to residents, falsifying records, manufacturing consent, or escaping current law and effect-specific authority. Sources: S1–S2.

Confucian, Legalist, and later imperial arguments were contested families of thought, not two operating-system choices. Remonstrance, humane rule, ritual, law, administrative technique, registers, rewards, and punishments were combined differently in practice. _Han Feizi_ is evidence of an argument for ruler-centered technique, not proof that fear is efficient or legitimate; the Han _Discourses on Salt and Iron_ records disagreement about monopoly, war finance, livelihood, and state reach rather than one Chinese position. Sources: S3 and E4.

The _Arthashastra_ maps treasury, territory, officials, armed capacity, alliances, intelligence, and welfare into statecraft. Its received text has layered and disputed dating and cannot be read as a transcript of one Mauryan government. Its attention to resources and interdependence may inform a diagnostic ledger; its secret, coercive, discriminatory, or lethal proposals do not become KINGDOM permissions. Sources: S4–S5.

## East Asian cases

### Zhou — layered lineage rule and a portable legitimacy language

- **Scope and evidence:** This entry is bounded to Western Zhou, conventionally c. 1045–771 BCE; later transmitted texts are evidence of reception as well as possible earlier material, not court transcripts. Administrative evidence is concentrated in elite bronze inscriptions, so neither source body is a census of ordinary life. Sources: E1–E2 and E20–E21.
- **Power:** A royal house worked through aristocratic lineages, regional states, ritual offices, and service appointments. Authority was layered and negotiated; it was not a modern federal constitution.
- **Fiscal and money:** Agricultural surplus, tribute, gifts, labor, military service, and control of land and bronze production mattered before coinage became widespread in the later period. Ritual objects also recorded office, reward, duty, and lineage standing.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Appointment and ritual vocabularies outlived the western court. A transmitted Zhou text frames Heaven's decree as transferable after a ruler's failure; that is evidence for a legitimacy argument, not proof that subjects could enforce a constitutional rule.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Hereditary rank, lineage privilege, labor obligations, warfare, and elite access to ritual and record sharply limited whose experience became authoritative.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Court conflict, the outward movement of constituent powers, and attacks on the western political core interacted before the royal court moved east in 771 BCE. That spatial and political break did not erase Zhou institutions.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Layered authority can carry shared norms across distance, but duties, review, appeal, and fiscal incidence must be explicit at every layer.
- **Non-inference:** Zhou duration does not prove hereditary hierarchy, cultural homogeneity, or a cycle of dynasties to be a law of history.

### Qin — concentrated mobilization and the brittleness of compulsory scale

- **Scope and evidence:** The Qin state developed for centuries; the unified empire lasted 221–206 BCE. Excavated legal and administrative manuscripts now qualify later hostile histories, but surviving records remain official and regionally incomplete. Sources: E3–E5 and E22–E23.
- **Power:** The court extended commanderies and counties staffed by appointed officials, standardized reporting, and household registration. Multiple fiscal offices existed even within the concentrated imperial structure.
- **Fiscal and money:** Field and poll taxes, grain, cash, conscription, labor service, accounts, granaries, and standardized weights and bronze coin enabled logistics and made households unusually legible to the state.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Qin standards and territorial administration were reused and revised by successors; the Qin ruling house itself did not survive the demands of its imperial mobilization.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Collective liability, harsh punishment, coerced migration and resettlement, convict and captive labor, conquest, and enormous projects imposed risk on registered households and captives.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Expansion raised supply and monitoring costs; court succession failure, elite conflict, extraction, and popular revolts interacted. “Harshness alone caused collapse” is too simple.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Standards, ledgers, and logistics increase capacity; capacity needs independent brakes, truthful burden reports, appeal, and a stop before mobilization consumes its constituency.
- **Non-inference:** Rapid unification does not prove concentrated coercion is efficient, just, culturally inherent, or suitable for present conflict.

### Han — revision, mixed administration, and arguments over fiscal reach

- **Scope and evidence:** Han rule, 206 BCE–220 CE with the Xin interruption, is documented by court histories, excavated records, texts, coins, and archaeology; local, frontier, enslaved, and women's voices remain unequal. Sources: E4–E5, E22, and E24.
- **Power:** A central court governed commanderies while managing kingdoms, local magnates, officials, and frontier commands. Recruitment and review grew more bureaucratic without ending family and patronage power.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land and poll taxes, labor and military obligations, grain storage, bronze cash, and at times salt, iron, and other monopolies financed administration and expansion.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Han rulers relaxed parts of Qin mobilization, retained useful administrative forms, debated monopoly and welfare, and repeatedly recomposed center–local bargains.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, private and government slavery, punitive law, conscription, labor obligations, and unequal household and legal status persisted beneath later narratives of ordered government.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Court and succession conflict, land and creditor concentration, fiscal-military burdens, epidemics, local armies, and rebellion combined; successor states retained many Han institutions.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** A successor can preserve interoperable infrastructure while revising its coercive objective; open fiscal argument is a correction channel, not evidence that correction prevailed.
- **Non-inference:** Han duration does not prove one bureaucracy, examination tradition, ethnic identity, or imperial boundary to be timeless.

### Tang — fiscal reform after the social facts changed

- **Scope and evidence:** Tang, 618–907, varied sharply before and after the mid-eighth century. Statutes, memorials, manuscripts, tax debates, and archaeology privilege literate administration but also expose disagreement. Sources: E6–E8 and E25.
- **Power:** The court worked through prefectures and counties, aristocratic and examination-linked officials, armies, monasteries, markets, and later powerful regional military governors.
- **Fiscal and money:** Early land-allocation assumptions supported taxes in grain, cloth, and labor. As landholding and registration changed, the 780 twice-yearly tax shifted assessment toward property and actual residence. Coin, textiles, grain, and other media coexisted.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Legal, urban, and transregional institutions absorbed religious and commercial diversity; explicit fiscal redesign acknowledged that the old tax map no longer matched the world.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, corvée, household registration, military extraction, forced loans, and unequal tax incidence burdened people who were weakly represented in court debate.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Rebellion, frontier war, the growing reach of regional armies, changing land control, and a shrinking central revenue base redistributed power long before formal dynastic termination.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** When ownership, mobility, or payment media change, fiscal rules require re-observation and contestable incidence—not forced compliance with a stale state model.
- **Non-inference:** One famous reform does not establish a universally fair tax, and cosmopolitan exchange does not erase coercion or frontier war.

### Song — fiscal monetization, civilian administration, and costly resilience

- **Scope and evidence:** Northern and Southern Song, 960–1279, occupied different territories and fiscal environments. Dense official records are unusually rich but still center literate state and elite concerns; this entry reads the 1005 Chanyuan settlement through a modern study that foregrounds both its durability and fragility. Sources: E9–E10, E26–E27, and E36.
- **Power:** A centrally coordinated court and large civil and military bureaucracies supervised circuits, prefectures, counties, monopolies, armies, and cities while negotiating local implementation through landholders and communities.
- **Fiscal and money:** Copper cash, iron coin in some regions, paper notes, silver, grain, cloth, property assessment, commercial taxes, and monopolies formed a regionally varied system. A money unit of account did not require every obligation to be paid in the same medium.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Administrative records, printing, market depth, hydraulic and urban systems, and fiscal experimentation supported recovery after territorial loss and a move of the capital south. The 1005 Chanyuan covenant made explicit a Song–Liao territorial status quo, diplomatic parity, reciprocal border restraints, and annual Song transfers of silver and silk; it ended decades of war and structured a long peace between those parties.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Peasant dues, forced or commuted service, monopoly burdens, unequal official privilege, and heavy military taxation complicate any image of a purely civilian commercial order.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Sustained defense costs, contested reforms, regional inequality, credit and currency pressures, and conquest by Jin and later Mongol forces changed the state without ending its social and administrative inheritances. Chanyuan's bilateral rules did not readily absorb Xi Xia aspirations, changing alliances, or new players; later Song policy abandoned the covenant.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Multiple payment rails can increase resilience when convertibility, regional burden, issuer liability, and failure modes are visible. Chanyuan also suggests that named parties can substitute explicit parity, transfers, territory, and reciprocal restraint for continued war, while a durable feedback loop must observe third-party interests and changed state.
- **Non-inference:** Chanyuan does not prove that payment for peace, diplomatic parity, bilateral demilitarization, or any treaty template is universally just or safe; paper money, markets, and learned officials likewise do not create trust, inclusion, or fiscal sustainability by themselves.

### Yuan — continental integration, plural intermediaries, and uneven currency

- **Scope and evidence:** Yuan rule in China, conventionally 1271–1368, was one part of a changing Mongol imperial world. Chinese official records must be read with Persian, Mongolian, archaeological, and local evidence. Sources: E9–E11 and E28–E29.
- **Power:** The imperial house governed through provinces, branches of the ruling family, military formations, inherited Chinese offices, and diverse Central and West Asian intermediaries; legal status was unequal.
- **Fiscal and money:** Paper currency, grain and silver taxes, salt monopoly income, corvée, appanages, and requisition coexisted. Local studies show shifts between coin, paper, and kind rather than one empire-wide linear monetization story.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Provincial administration and long-distance routes linked regions, while institutional borrowing allowed rapid rule of heterogeneous territories.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, legally differentiated status, requisition, corvée, war-captive slavery, and fiscal privilege made integration radically unequal; their scale and local forms remain unevenly recoverable.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Paper-money instability, disasters, court and succession conflict, fiscal fragmentation, military rivalry, and rebellions interacted; the imperial house and Yuan political lineages continued north of the former capital after 1368.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Shared rails need locally observable value, bounded issuance, redemption or fallback, and equal standing across intermediary classes.
- **Non-inference:** Connectivity does not prove consent, and neither paper issuance nor ethnic identity supplies a single-cause collapse explanation.

### Ming — agrarian registers meeting a silver-commercial world

- **Scope and evidence:** Ming, 1368–1644, changed substantially across three centuries. Official compilations, local gazetteers, contracts, foreign trade records, and archaeology illuminate different fiscal levels. Sources: E11–E12 and E30–E32.
- **Power:** An emperor-centered civil bureaucracy relied on provinces, counties, local elites, hereditary military households, palace institutions, and community registration with a thin official presence per resident.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land and grain taxes, labor service, salt and customs income, military provisioning, and early paper notes gave way to widespread silver accounting and commutation, associated with the Single Whip reforms.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Surveys, granaries, courier systems, local compacts, commercial networks, and conversion of complex dues into silver adapted an agrarian design without fully rebuilding its fiscal center.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Corvée, hereditary military status, coerced resettlement, frontier war, imperial purges, and burdens passed through local brokers remained substantial.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Silver valuation and liquidity, escalating military expenditure, tax resistance, rebellion, and Manchu state-building combined; a sudden cutoff of foreign silver is not a sufficient explanation.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Commutation can simplify a ledger while transferring exchange-rate and liquidity risk to the payer; record who bears that risk before calling the reform efficient.
- **Non-inference:** Silver did not mechanically destroy Ming, and a thin center is neither inherently restrained nor inherently incapable.

### Qing — broad territorial rule with constrained fiscal depth

- **Scope and evidence:** Qing, founded 1636 and ruling Beijing from 1644 to 1912, governed changing Inner Asian and Chinese territories. Archives are extensive but multilingual, unevenly open, and shaped by imperial and local record practices. Sources: E13–E15 and E33.
- **Power:** The imperial house combined banner institutions, civil bureaucracy, provincial government, local gentry and lineages, frontier arrangements, and multiple legal-administrative repertoires.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land taxes largely constrained by inherited quotas, silver and copper cash, salt, customs, tribute, local surcharges, later maritime customs, foreign loans, and indemnities linked fiscal capacity to local knowledge and external creditors.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Combined civil and military institutions, negotiated local collection, routinized transfers among provincial and central treasuries, and later provincial fiscal-military initiative sustained vast and changing domains.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest warfare, banner privilege and segregation, military requisition, and burdens hidden in collection intermediaries cannot be netted against duration.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Population and administrative mismatch, constrained revenue, corruption and private court extraction, rebellion, imperial warfare, foreign coercion, indemnities, regional armies, and reform conflicts redistributed effective power.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Low headline tax can conceal low public capacity and high informal incidence; custody, audit, and beneficiary maps matter more than a nominal rate.
- **Non-inference:** Qing history does not establish a timeless Chinese fiscal weakness, one ethnic conflict, or “tradition versus modernity” as destiny.

### Tokugawa — nested rule, standardized status, and commercial transformation

- **Scope and evidence:** Tokugawa rule, 1603–1868, joined a shogunal center to varied domains. Domain archives, village documents, merchant accounts, law, material evidence, and later narratives reveal unequal local detail. Sources: E16–E19 and E34–E35.
- **Power:** The bakuhan order distributed authority among shogunate, daimyo domains, villages, towns, temples and shrines, while alternate attendance and marriage or castle controls constrained regional lords.
- **Fiscal and money:** Rice-based land assessment and taxes coexisted with gold, silver, and copper coin, domain notes, merchant credit, tolls, and market exchange. Domains faced expenses in cash even when revenue was denominated in rice.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Nested administration, roads, castle towns, household and village responsibility, literacy, and domain experimentation supported long internal coordination.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Formal status distinctions, village burdens, discrimination against outcaste associations, household registration, and coercive attendance costs shaped that order.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Commercial wealth diverged from official status and rice revenue; debt, famine, domain reform, rural protest, intellectual change, and foreign military pressure altered coalitions.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Polycentric systems can experiment locally while sharing standards, but every local center also needs a rights floor, appeal beyond itself, and transparent cross-level obligations.
- **Non-inference:** Tokugawa duration does not validate inherited status, maritime restriction, alternate attendance, or a myth of complete seclusion.

## West, Central, and South Asian imperial cases

### Achaemenid — imperial standards with negotiated regional administration

- **Scope and evidence:** The Achaemenid Empire, c. 550–330 BCE, is known through royal inscriptions, administrative tablets, archaeology, coinage, and often adversarial Greek accounts. No one archive represents all regions. Sources: W1–W3.
- **Power:** The king worked through satraps, royal inspectors, military commanders, local dynasts, temples, cities, and inherited administrative languages and law; provinces were neither autonomous equals nor identical command units.
- **Fiscal and money:** Assessed tribute and tax arrived in silver and kind, alongside labor, livestock, grain, land revenue, customs, and gifts. Royal gold coin existed within plural regional monetary practices.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Roads, posts, archives, calibrated regional accommodation, and reuse of earlier institutions enabled coordination across ecological, linguistic, and legal difference.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, punitive expeditions, elite privilege, extractive tribute, and debt slavery documented in Babylonia remain part of the same system often praised for “tolerance.”
- **Pressures and transformation:** Succession conflict, satrapal rivalry, revolts, court and military costs, and Macedonian conquest interacted; administrative practices continued under successor regimes.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Interoperable standards need not erase local custody, but accommodation is legitimate only with standing, reply, exit or appeal—not merely because extraction is delegated.
- **Non-inference:** The case does not prove benign empire, fixed satrapies, or cultural tolerance as an Achaemenid essence.

### Roman imperial West — city-mediated fiscal power and unequal expanding citizenship

- **Scope and evidence:** This entry examines the Principate and later western imperial system, c. 27 BCE–fifth century CE; it does not compress the Republic, eastern continuation, or every province into one “Rome.” Law, inscriptions, papyri, coins, archaeology, and elite literature preserve different provinces unevenly. Sources: W4–W5 and W19–W21.
- **Power:** Emperors, Senate, magistrates, army, governors, city councils, tax agents, landowners, and households redistributed authority repeatedly. Local civic elites translated imperial demand into provincial collection and order.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land and poll taxes, customs, rents, mines, tribute, requisition, coinage, and later more in-kind supply supported army, bureaucracy, courts, roads, cities, and distributions.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Municipal incorporation, selective and later broad grants of citizenship, law, roads, ports, records, and recruitment of provincial elites made imperial institutions locally reproducible without making political standing equal.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, systemic slavery, provincial extraction, dispossession, spectacle, and unequal legal status were structural, not incidental externalities.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Civil war and succession, army payroll, epidemic, currency and tax change, frontier conflict, migration, and divergent regional coalitions transformed the western system.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Local intermediaries can lower coordination costs while hiding incidence and capture; audit both the center's demand and the intermediary's extraction.
- **Non-inference:** Neither roads nor citizenship redeem conquest, and 476 is not a universal date when Roman society, law, or state practice vanished.

### Byzantine / East Roman — survival by reconfiguration after territorial shock

- **Scope and evidence:** “Byzantine” here names the medieval East Roman state, especially c. 330–1453; its inhabitants often called it Roman. Evidence is rich for court, church, law, seals, and coin but uneven for region and period. Sources: W5–W8 and W22.
- **Power:** Emperor, fiscal and judicial ministries, provinces or themes, army, church, cities, landholders, and later negotiated commercial powers formed changing rather than fixed layers.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land and hearth assessment, customs, coin and in-kind levies, monopolies, state property, and military supply were reorganized repeatedly. The gold solidus/nomisma gave a long-lived accounting and payment anchor without eliminating fiscal change.
- **Durability and adaptation:** After seventh-century territorial loss, the state reduced scale, changed provincial-military organization, preserved fiscal and legal records, and later expanded again.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Tax burdens, war, forced movement, land concentration, and unequal legal and economic standing qualify narratives of resilient continuity.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Epidemic, civil conflict, territorial and revenue loss, changes in the agrarian base, and disruption or redirection of commercial routes repeatedly narrowed resources and options.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Durability can mean shrinking, renaming, handing over, and preserving critical functions—not maximizing territory or pretending the pre-shock state can be restored.
- **Non-inference:** A stable high-value coin, religious identity, or imperial office is neither a sufficient cause of survival nor authority to persist.

### Abbasid — specialized administration, provincial bargaining, symbolic afterlife

- **Scope and evidence:** Abbasid caliphal rule began in 750; direct central control varied long before Baghdad's capture in 1258, and Abbasid caliphal symbolism continued in Cairo. Administrative manuals, chronicles, papyri, coins, and archaeology are socially and regionally uneven. Sources: W9–W12 and W23.
- **Power:** Caliph, household, vizier, specialized diwans, judges, tax officials, governors, military formations, cities, landholders, scholars, and later regional dynasties divided practical and symbolic authority.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land tax, differentiated personal or alms obligations, customs, coin, estates, tribute, tax farming, and later revenue assignments financed court, public works, patronage, and a professional military.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Inherited Sasanian and Byzantine practices, Arabic documentation, specialized bureaus, trade networks, and mobile scholarly institutions carried capacity beyond direct caliphal control.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, persistent and varied slavery, military coercion, status-differentiated burdens, and court violence accompanied celebrated learning and commerce.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Provincial retention of revenue, agricultural and irrigation change, military appropriation, court expense, faction, and Buyid, Seljuk, and Mongol power changed the center in stages.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Separate ledgers and offices can make custody inspectable, but a specialist bureau becomes a rival sovereign when funding, coercion, and review collapse into its own hands.
- **Non-inference:** “Golden age,” “decline,” and “Islamic civilisation” are not unitary explanations, periods, actors, or value judgments.

### Ottoman — negotiated incorporation, revenue delegation, and creditor power

- **Scope and evidence:** The Ottoman polity developed from the late thirteenth century and ended as an empire after the First World War. Its institutions varied across six centuries and three continents; archives are abundant but never a view from every subject. Sources: W13–W15, W18, and W24–W25.
- **Power:** Sultan and household, palace and scribal bureaus, judges and religious establishments, military corps, provinces, vassals, communities, guilds, tax farmers, and later local notables and creditors shared unequal and changing authority.
- **Fiscal and money:** Land-revenue assignments, cash taxes, customs, monopolies, tax farms, waqf holdings, coin and debasement, domestic finance, and later foreign debt connected armed capacity to intermediaries.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Legal and administrative pluralism, bargaining with regional power, professional records, recurrent fiscal redesign, and incorporation of new elites allowed substantial adaptation.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, slavery, devshirme, unequal status, punitive taxation, dispossession, repression, and late imperial mass violence—including genocide—cannot be converted into a durability benefit.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Changing military costs and trade, tax-farm entrenchment, provincial bargaining, nationalist projects, creditor and imperial intervention, refugees, reform conflict, and total war interacted.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Revenue delegation creates constitutional power. Its term, price, collection methods, appeal, audit, and reversion must be explicit before it becomes difficult to revoke.
- **Non-inference:** Ottoman longevity does not validate empire, communal hierarchy, tax farming, coerced recruitment, or a timeless East–West clash.

### Mughal — ranked service, assigned revenue, and plural local brokerage

- **Scope and evidence:** Mughal rule began in 1526; effective imperial reach varied well before formal termination in 1857. Court chronicles, revenue manuals, orders, contracts, regional texts, coins, and Company archives carry distinct political biases. Sources: W16–W17 and W26–W27.
- **Power:** Emperor and court ranked mansabdars, assigned jagirs, and worked through provincial officials, zamindars, local rulers, merchants, religious institutions, villages, and mobile military labor.
- **Fiscal and money:** Agrarian assessment was central, payable through variable cash and kind arrangements; the silver rupee, customs, tribute, minting, revenue assignments, and credit linked local production to mobile officials and imperial expenditure.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Surveys and revenue schedules, transferable service rank, Persianate records, negotiated local sovereignty, and commercial depth widened the coalition without making it equal.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest and plunder, heavy or opportunistic collection, agrarian hierarchy, court war, and power delegated to extractive brokers made plural rule unequal.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Succession wars, jagir and rank pressure, regional military-fiscal powers, agrarian resistance, faction, changing trade and credit, and Company expansion dispersed imperial capacity.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Rotating an intermediary can constrain local sovereignty yet shorten its incentive horizon and intensify extraction; evaluate payer experience, not only central control.
- **Non-inference:** Mughal history is neither one ruler's tolerance story nor proof of religious incompatibility, hydraulic despotism, or inevitable colonial replacement.

### Chola — inscription-rich nested administration, irrigation, and merchant power

- **Scope and evidence:** The imperial Chola period, roughly ninth to thirteenth centuries CE, centered on Tamil regions and extended through war and trade across South India and the Indian Ocean. Inscriptions are abundant but disproportionately record royal, temple, landholder, and donor acts. Sources: A1–A3 and A15–A16.
- **Power:** Kings and officers worked through mandalam and valanadu divisions, nadu organizations, village bodies such as ur and sabha, urban nagaram, temples, military chiefs, and transregional merchant guilds.
- **Fiscal and money:** Measured land and produce, dues in cash and kind, customs, occupational levies, labor, irrigation obligations, tax exemptions, temple endowments, and merchant credit linked local assemblies to the court.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Irrigation maintenance, written land and gift records, nested locality, temple-centered storage and redistribution, and merchant networks coordinated agrarian and maritime circuits.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, occupation, and organized plunder in Sri Lanka, warfare, landholding and tax-exemption privilege, coerced burdens, and elite control of many assemblies qualify “self-government.”
- **Pressures and transformation:** Succession, costly campaigns, changing regional coalitions, Pandya and other rivals, shifts in trade and land power, and local institutional autonomy redistributed Chola authority.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Nested councils and infrastructure custody can preserve local knowledge, but membership, affected non-members, appeal, tax exemption incidence, and maintenance labor must remain visible.
- **Non-inference:** Temple inscriptions do not prove inclusive village democracy, and maritime reach does not turn conquest into benign trade.

## African cases

### Aksum and Ethiopian highland states — institutional lineage without seamless identity

- **Scope and evidence:** Aksum flourished in present-day northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, especially c. first–seventh centuries CE. This entry also notes selected later Zagwe and Solomonic highland institutions; it does not claim one uninterrupted state or ethnic subject. Coins, inscriptions, archaeology, chronicles, land grants, and oral traditions leave major gaps. Sources: A4–A7.
- **Power:** Aksumite kings linked a highland core to Red Sea routes and local rulers. Later highland monarchies relied variably on nobles, military leaders, the Orthodox Church, monasteries, regional communities, and land grants, with recurring center–regional contest.
- **Fiscal and money:** Aksum issued gold, silver, and copper coin while trade, tribute, agriculture, and in-kind dues also mattered. Later rist land claims, gult revenue rights, tribute, labor, tithes, and market exchange distributed fiscal and judicial power through intermediaries.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Ge'ez textual practice, religious and monastic institutions, land memory, regional networks, and the capacity to relocate political centers carried identities and governance repertoires beyond Aksum's urban contraction.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, slavery, tribute and labor burdens, religious and occupational hierarchy, gendered land access, and later imperial dispossession in the south are part of the long record.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Red Sea route and political change, ecological stress, regional realignment, religious and military conflict, noble decentralization, invasion, and later centralization acted differently across periods; Aksum's decline remains evidentially contested.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Archives, language, and distributed institutions can preserve continuity when a capital or fiscal circuit ends; distinguish institutional inheritance from identity or ownership claims.
- **Non-inference:** No modern people, boundary, church, or government gains exclusive authority by asserting a simple, seamless descent from Aksum.

### Mali and Songhai — river, caravan, agrarian, and scholarly networks

- **Scope and evidence:** Mali and Songhai were distinct, changing West African imperial formations, especially c. thirteenth–sixteenth centuries. Arabic chronicles and travelers, oral traditions, archaeology, and later European records must be read together without treating one as neutral. Sources: A8–A10.
- **Power:** Courts and armies worked through provinces, tributary rulers, lineages, Niger towns, farming communities, merchants, and Muslim scholarly institutions; practical reach varied by route and season.
- **Fiscal and money:** Gold, salt, agriculture, livestock, customs, tribute, market dues, labor, cowries, gold dust, and other exchange media supported political and commercial networks. Neither polity was merely a gold mine.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Control of river and caravan nodes, local brokerage, agricultural surplus, scholarly cities, diplomatic exchange, and multiple media connected environments without one uniform administration.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Conquest, enslavement and slave trading, tribute, hierarchy, war, and unequal access to literacy and long-distance commerce accompanied wealth and learning.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Succession and civil conflict, provincial rivalry, ecological and route change, shifting trade, firearms, and the 1591 Moroccan invasion combined; communities and commerce persisted afterward.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** A network survives by serving its producing communities as well as taxing its chokepoints; map route substitution, ecological dependence, and who bears security costs.
- **Non-inference:** Wealth does not equal coined money, gold does not explain a whole polity, and conquest did not erase West African political knowledge.

## Indigenous American cases

### Inka — labor taxation, reciprocal obligation, records, and logistical concentration

- **Scope and evidence:** Tawantinsuyu expanded rapidly in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries across the Andes. Archaeology, khipu research, landscapes, Indigenous memory, and colonial accounts—often written through invasion—must be triangulated. Sources: A11–A12 and A17–A18.
- **Power:** The Sapa Inka and court organized four suyus through provincial officials, local kurakas, ayllu communities, resettled groups, specialists, roads, storehouses, runners, and khipu record keepers.
- **Fiscal and money:** There was no state-minted coinage. Mit'a labor, agricultural and craft production, herd and land access, storage, feasting, redistribution, and reciprocal obligation moved value and risk.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Roads, bridges, ecological complementarity, distributed storehouses, local brokerage, and inspectable account practices enabled supply across difficult terrain.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Imperial conquest, compulsory labor, forced resettlement, hierarchy, appropriation, and punishment mean reciprocity was not always freely chosen or equally balanced.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Succession war, epidemic and demographic shock, contested provinces, concentrated command, and Spanish invasion with Indigenous allies produced collapse of the imperial center, not Andean life.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Reserves and non-cash contribution can build resilience only when duty, reciprocity, bodily risk, refusal, and the community's own account of value are explicit.
- **Non-inference:** Coin is not required for complex coordination, but the absence of coin does not prove equality, consent, or freedom from extraction.

### Haudenosaunee Confederacy — nested sovereignty, consensus, and living continuity

- **Scope and evidence:** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy predates sustained European settlement; exact founding chronology is debated. It joined five, later six, nations and remains living. Oral law and community authority take priority; colonial transcriptions and outside scholarship are mediated records, not ownership of the tradition. Sources: A13–A14.
- **Power:** Nations retain distinct councils and responsibilities within a Grand Council process; clan mothers select and may remove titled leaders, while clans, families, speakers, and consensual deliberation distribute roles without creating one centralized sovereign.
- **Fiscal and money:** Agriculture, hunting, sharing, gift and diplomatic exchange, trade, and wampum supported social and political relations. Wampum records commitments and relationships; reducing it to European-style currency erases functions and custody.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Confederated autonomy, oral and material constitutional memory, condolence and repair practices, adoption, diplomacy, and deliberation sustained continuity through enormous external pressure.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Warfare, captivity and adoption practices, role distinctions, internal disagreement, and unequal encounters must be studied in period context, without romanticizing or importing a state template.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Epidemic, settler colonial war, dispossession, treaty violation, imposed borders and councils, boarding schools, and economic coercion fractured territory but did not terminate Haudenosaunee sovereignty or community.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** A center can coordinate without owning its members; durable confederation requires retained local voice, recall, patient consensus, memory custody, and a repair protocol.
- **Non-inference:** The case is not a primitive utopia, proof of one disputed influence claim about the United States, or a design resource detachable from living Haudenosaunee authority.

## European corporate and polycentric contrasts

### Venice — constrained executive, oligarchic records, and public debt

- **Scope and evidence:** The Venetian Republic developed over centuries and ended in 1797. This entry emphasizes the later medieval and early modern maritime-fiscal state; government and merchant archives are rich while subject, laboring, and colonial voices are less evenly preserved. Sources: P1–P2 and P8.
- **Power:** Doge, Great Council, Senate, magistracies, courts, patrician families, guilds, mainland and maritime governors, merchants, and creditors formed a checked but deeply oligarchic system.
- **Fiscal and money:** Customs, consumption and property taxes, monopolies, colonial revenue, forced loans, funded public debt, coin, and emergency finance tied affluent citizens and later investors to state survival.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Collegial offices, rotation, records, diplomacy, naval logistics, credible debt service, and repeated fiscal innovation limited some personal rule and supported long coordination.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Political closure, regressive incidence, colonial conquest, subject inequality, imperial war, and creditor privilege financed the admired stability.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Trade-route competition, plague, war costs, territorial commitments, oligarchic rigidity, and rival naval-commercial powers narrowed options before the republic ended.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Debt credibility depends on custody, audit, incidence, representation, and a believable future revenue stream; it can finance public goods or lock future payers into elite priorities.
- **Non-inference:** Executive checks within an oligarchy do not establish democratic standing, just taxation, or benign commercial expansion.

### Dutch Republic — federated bargaining, creditor representation, and colonial extraction

- **Scope and evidence:** The Republic of the Seven United Netherlands, c. 1588–1795, was a federation of unequal provinces and cities, not a unitary modern nation-state. Fiscal and commercial archives are dense but reflect propertied institutions and chartered companies. Sources: P2–P4 and P9.
- **Power:** Provinces, city regents, States General, stadholder, admiralty boards, local tax offices, merchants, bondholders, and chartered companies divided authority through delegation, instruction, and veto.
- **Fiscal and money:** Excises, customs, provincial taxes and debt, city credit, transferable securities, and company finance supported war and infrastructure. Holland's wealth and liabilities dominated an unequal union.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Taxpayer and creditor monitoring, local collection capacity, liquid debt markets, urban records, and federal bargaining produced exceptional borrowing capacity.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Regressive consumption taxes, oligarchic closure, colonial conquest, slavery, monopoly violence, dispossession, and corporate coercion transferred costs far outside represented creditors.
- **Pressures and transformation:** War costs, provincial free-riding and asymmetry, commercial rivalry, regent–stadholder conflict, debt, popular exclusion, and French invasion changed the federation.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Representation by those who lend and pay can constrain default, but affected parties outside the fiscal chamber need standing, veto or appeal, and a full externality ledger.
- **Non-inference:** Credible markets are not democracy, and financial sophistication does not launder colonial or corporate violence.

### Holy Roman Empire — negotiated order without a sovereign fiscal center

- **Scope and evidence:** The Holy Roman Empire, conventionally 962–1806, changed profoundly across a millennium. Legal acts, diets, court records, city and territorial archives, and diplomatic reports illuminate a polity whose boundaries and membership were never static. Sources: P5–P7.
- **Power:** Emperor, electors, territorial princes, bishops, free cities, estates, Imperial Diet, courts, and after 1500 Imperial Circles distributed authority. No layer maps cleanly to a modern federal office.
- **Fiscal and money:** Most taxation, coinage, debt, tolls, estates, and military finance were territorial or urban. Imperial levies and troop contributions depended on negotiated registers and member compliance.
- **Durability and adaptation:** Consensus, mediation, courts, corporate privilege, overlapping identity, and Circle coordination allowed local autonomy and common action without continuous direct central rule.
- **Harms and exclusions:** Serfdom, corporate and religious privilege, confessional coercion, fragmented protection, local oligarchy, and severe wartime extraction show the cost of leaving rights mostly territorial.
- **Pressures and transformation:** Reformation, the Thirty Years' War, dynastic rivalry, member militarization, uneven fiscal capacity, external powers, and Napoleonic restructuring repeatedly changed the bargain.
- **Bounded KINGDOM inference:** Constitutional ambiguity and distributed authority can support negotiated coexistence, but common rights, emergency duties, funding, and final appeal cannot remain implicit.
- **Non-inference:** Decentralization is not automatically peaceful, free, or weak; the Empire's duration does not make every veto or privilege valuable.

## Comparative findings: recurrent mechanisms, never a recipe

These associations recur across dissimilar cases. They are hypotheses for closer inspection, not scores, laws of civilisation, or permissions.

1. **Layered rule scales and conceals.** Satrapies, city councils, domains, provinces, jagirs, gult, villages, and confederated nations preserve local knowledge. They can also hide extraction or create local tyranny. A durable design therefore needs both local custody and an appeal beyond the local custodian.
2. **Records create capacity and exposure.** Registers, accounts, khipu, inscriptions, archives, surveys, and budgets coordinate resources and make correction possible. They also make households taxable, conscriptable, and punishable. Collect only what an accepted purpose needs, separate audit from world state, and bind access to current authority.
3. **The tax base shapes the bargaining chamber.** A center dependent on taxpayers or creditors often must negotiate with some of them. A center funded by tribute, plunder, monopoly rent, foreign indemnity, or a captive resource can evade reciprocity. Neither arrangement guarantees voice for laborers, subjects, colonies, or future payers.
4. **Intermediaries receive power, not just work.** A tax farmer, jagirdar, gult-holder, city council, temple, guild, noble, or chartered company gains information, revenue, enforcement leverage, and often jurisdiction. Treat delegation as an expiring constitutional grant with incidence, audit, appeal, revocation, and repair.
5. **Payment media redistribute risk.** Coin, paper, silver, grain, labor, cowries, credit, and mixed payment are not a ladder of progress. Conversion shifts price, transport, storage, liquidity, bodily, and default risks among issuer, collector, payer, and beneficiary.
6. **Debt extends today's coalition into tomorrow.** Credible public debt can mobilize emergency resources and bind officials to audit. It can also give creditors priority over subsistence, public repair, colonies, and people not represented when the debt was accepted.
7. **Reserves and infrastructure buy time, not legitimacy.** Roads, canals, granaries, storehouses, ports, postal routes, and financial reserves widen feasible choices under shock. Their construction, custody, ecological burden, access, and military use decide whom that resilience serves.
8. **Succession is infrastructure.** Repeated court wars, purges, and fiscal seizures show the cost of treating leadership transfer as a personal event. A durable institution separates role, person, assets, records, interim authority, and challenge process.
9. **Pluralism can be accommodation or ranked containment.** Retaining local law, language, religion, or office can support coexistence. If standing and appeal remain unequal, the same arrangement preserves domination more efficiently. Diversity must not mean permanent inherited jurisdiction over another being.
10. **Bad news is a strategic resource.** Fiscal debates, remonstrance, petitions, local records, assemblies, and revolt all reveal mismatch. Systems that punish messengers learn late; systems that merely archive complaint without remedy only simulate feedback.
11. **War expands extractive pathways.** Logistics and emergency finance can produce administrative capacity, debt, standardization, and central power. These pathways persist after danger. Every emergency power needs exact scope, separate custody, review, expiry, demobilization, and repair.
12. **Transformation is normal.** Durable lineages shrink, federate, translate, move capitals, change media, lose territory, share sovereignty, or end. Sustainability is the preservation of legitimate viable choices—including handover and completion—not the indefinite survival of one center.

## A bounded encounter worksheet

Before invoking a historical analogy for a current encounter, record:

```text
current parties and each party's own description
interaction type: exchange | interdependence | incompatibility | coercion
                  | armed_attack | unknown
claim kind: observation | interpretation | expectation | commitment
historical boundary and the present feature alleged to match
material differences, missing parties, and evidence quality
power topology: decision | money | force | information | appeal | exit
fiscal circuit: payer | medium | collector | beneficiary | externality
affected non-parties, future payers, ecological burden, and repair owed
peaceful and reversible options tried or still available
current law, authority, expiry, review, halt, and record custodian
what evidence would falsify the analogy
stop: no analogy dispatches an action or widens authority
```

An analogy that cannot fill these fields remains an invitation to investigate. It is not a strategy, prediction, enemy model, or authorization.

## Closing boundary

This atlas supports one disciplined move: compare named mechanisms under named limits, then return to present evidence and present authority. It does not identify enemies, forecast destiny, excuse pre-emption, allocate collective guilt, prescribe conquest, or teach operational violence. No civilisation is one being. No duration is a reward. No historical association is a key.
